### Coffee Tasting for Beginners: What to Expect

Start a tasting by putting two cups side by side so you can compare them. Smell each coffee before taking a sip. Start with familiar references such as orange peel, cocoa, toasted nuts or black tea. If nothing comes to mind, describe the difference you notice. One cup might smell stronger, feel lighter, or leave a longer aftertaste.

Taste the same coffees again as they cool. Try noticing sweetness and mouthfeel before deciding which cup you prefer. Two people can agree that a coffee smells fruity and still prefer different cups. That difference makes for a better conversation about what you would buy or brew at home.

In **Concept:Tasting**, you compare specialty coffees with an instructor and discuss how where they were grown, how they were processed, and how they were roasted can influence the cup. The session takes 90 minutes and welcomes beginners. Bring a coffee you know well to mind so you have a familiar point of comparison.

### Planning a Coffee Workshop for a Large Group

Decide what you want everyone to be able to do at the same time. At a guided tasting, people can compare cups and answer the same prompt together. Hands-on espresso is different. One person at a time uses a group head or steam wand, so the number of workstations helps determine how much practice each person gets.

Give the studio your expected headcount, venue, preferred language and time window. Let the instructor know if your group will be seated in a single room or moving between stations, how much setup time the schedule allows, and whether tables, power and water are available. For an off-site session, add arrival information and any access constraints.

Choose one shared activity to anchor the session. This might be comparing two coffees, watching the effect of grind size on espresso, or collecting a few tasting notes. Ask the studio to confirm the activity, timing and venue before you schedule it into the rest of the event.

### Why Coffee Makes a Great Team-Building Activity

A coffee tasting gives a team a shared problem with several reasonable answers. Ask everyone to smell the same coffee and write down one word before discussing it. One person might notice roasted nuts. Someone else might pick up a fruit note. Compare the observations before anyone says which cup they like best. The exercise invites participation without relying on prior knowledge.

For a small team, espresso practice can make for a different kind of collaboration. One person changes the grind, another times the shot, and a third compares the taste. Run a second round with just one change so the group can talk about what actually made a difference.

If you’re planning an office activity, tell the instructor whether you want a seated tasting, a demonstration, or hands-on practice. Share the team size, available space and time. Ask how the session will be adapted for your venue and participants.

### The Science of Tasting Coffee

Take a sip of coffee with your nose gently pinched, then release it. Pay attention to which sensations are present before you release your nose and which become more obvious afterward. Your sense of smell contributes to much of the character you recognize as flavor. Your tongue also detects tastes such as sweetness, sourness and bitterness. Texture comes from how the coffee feels in your mouth.

Try the same two coffees at a few temperatures. Write down observations before preferences. “Has a citrus aroma” describes what you perceived. “I prefer this cup” records your response to it. Keeping those notes separate lets you compare perceptions with someone whose tastes differ from yours.

Coffee professionals use controlled preparation and a shared vocabulary to make comparisons easier. A useful first experiment is to brew two coffees with the same recipe, then see how many differences you can describe without reaching for a score.
